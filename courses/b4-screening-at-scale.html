Screening at scale

b3 gave you a search that finds the good sources on purpose. Tonight the pile arrives and you have to decide what gets in - which is the step everybody skips, because reading feels like progress and excluding feels like loss. This session owns the hardest lesson in the track: more sources is not more evidence. You will write inclusion rules before you look, borrow PRISMA's screening log without its ceremony, run a retraction check that costs almost nothing, and then turn the anti-lever on from a clean audit and watch 40 extra sources cost you a claim.

Part 1 · rules before you look

Inclusion rules written before you look 8 min live

A rule set is one short page you write while the pile is still theoretical. It says who counts, when counts, what kind of source counts, in what language, and what has to be measured for a source to be usable at all. Write it first and screening becomes clerical work you can hand off, log and defend. Write it afterwards and you have done retroactive rule-making, which is a polite name for choosing the rules that keep the sources that agree with you. Nobody does this deliberately. Everybody does it accidentally, because by the time you look, you already have an answer in your head and every borderline call quietly bends toward it.

LiveWhat a rule set actually contains4 min▶

Six lines, written before the first search result loads. For the Cadence question they read something like this:

  • Population - higher education students and institutions. Not K-12, not corporate learning, not general consumers. Name what is out as well as what is in.
  • Date window, with a stated reason - 2021 onward, because the procurement and consent landscape we care about post-dates remote-teaching normalisation. The reason matters more than the year: a window with no rationale is a preference.
  • Study or source type - peer-reviewed studies, regulator or ministry guidance, and institutional procurement documents. Vendor material may be read for context and marked as such, never as support.
  • Language - English plus our two target markets. Write it down even when it is obvious, because it is a real limitation of your evidence base and you will need it in the write-up.
  • Outcome or measure required - the source has to report something about adoption, budget authority, consent practice or transcription performance. A source with an opinion and no measure does not clear the bar.
  • Exclusions - the mirror of the above, stated positively so a second person could apply them: no K-12-only samples, nothing before 2021, no vendor material as support, no source without a measure.

Now the test. Once written, the rule set is allowed to change - but only in one direction: you may make it stricter, and you record the change and the date. Loosening a rule after you have seen the pile is how an excluded source becomes an included one because you liked its conclusion.

Self-studyPRISMA 2020, borrowed without the ceremony4 min read▶

PRISMA 2020 (Page MJ et al., BMJ 2021;372:n71) is a reporting guideline, not a quality-appraisal tool - it tells you what to disclose, not whether a study is any good. Its checklist has seven sections with 27 items, some with sub-items: Title (1), Abstract (2), Introduction (3-4), Methods (5-15), Results (16-22), Discussion (23), Other information (24-27). New in the 2020 revision: certainty assessment, data and code availability, and a separate abstract checklist.

The part worth stealing for commercial work is the flow diagram, and the detail people get wrong is its shape. It has three bands, not four:

  • Identification - what you found, and what you removed as duplicates before screening anything.
  • Screening - records screened on title and abstract, records excluded, reports sought for retrieval, reports not retrieved, and reports assessed for eligibility with the exclusions and their reasons. The 2009 diagram had a separate "Eligibility" band; in 2020 it was removed and the eligibility assessment became a box inside Screening.
  • Included - the studies that made it, which is the set your claims are allowed to rest on.

There are four template variants (new versus updated review, databases-only versus with other sources). You do not need the templates, the checklist or the word "PRISMA" anywhere near a client deck. You need the log: a sheet with found, deduped, screened, excluded-with-reason, retrieved, assessed, included, and a total that adds up. Ten minutes of bookkeeping that turns "we looked at a lot of sources" into a number somebody else could reproduce.

Part 2 · the mechanics

Screening a big pile without reading all of it 7 min live

Three passes, always in this order. Dedupe first - the same study arriving from three databases is one study, and if you screen before you dedupe you will make the same judgement three times and sometimes make it differently. Then title and abstract, against the rules, fast, with a recorded reason on every exclusion. Then full text for the survivors only, which is where you find out that a third of the abstracts promised something the paper does not deliver. The pass structure is what makes the volume tractable: you never read 988 papers, you read 988 abstracts and 75 papers.

LiveAI-assisted triage that stays auditable5 min▶

Screening is genuinely a good use of a model - it is repetitive, rule-shaped and enormous. It stops being a good use the moment the tool is deciding what the rules are, or dropping things silently. Four conditions keep it defensible:

  • The tool applies your rules, not its judgement. Paste the rule set in. "Is this relevant?" is not a rule.
  • A reason per exclusion, in the tool's output. If it cannot say which rule a record failed, you cannot log it and you cannot defend it.
  • Sample-check its decisions against your own. Screen 40 records yourself, blind, and compare. Disagreements are usually a rule you wrote ambiguously, which is useful either way.
  • Nothing gets dropped silently. Excluded is a status with a reason attached, never an absence from a list.
Silent dropping

"Here are 400 abstracts on AI note-taking in education. Filter out the irrelevant ones and give me the papers worth reading."

Auditable triage

"Apply these inclusion rules exactly as written, and do not add criteria of your own: [paste the six rules]. For all 400 records return a row each with: record id, INCLUDE / EXCLUDE / UNCLEAR, and for every EXCLUDE the single rule number it failed. Use UNCLEAR whenever the abstract does not contain enough information to apply a rule - do not guess, and do not omit any record from the output. Return the counts for each of the three statuses at the end."

The UNCLEAR bucket is the part that earns its keep. It is where the abstract is silent about population or measure - and those are exactly the records a confident filter would have quietly binned. You read those yourself.

Self-studyThe limitation that bites hardest at this exact stage3 min read▶

Elicit aggregates Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex and PubMed, deduped to roughly 138M papers, and has a workflow built for this job (Systematic Literature Review, plus Extract Data for what we do in b5). The Pro tier screens up to 5,000 papers. The limitation to know before you trust a screening decision: full-text analysis only happens for open access material (or where you supply your own subscription plus their browser extension). Otherwise it works from title and abstract only.

Notice where that lands. Title-and-abstract screening is exactly the pass where "we could not tell from the abstract" is the correct answer a third of the time - and a tool without the full text cannot ever resolve it, so it has to guess or defer. Deferring is fine. Guessing looks identical in the output.

Second thing worth knowing, and it is a general lesson rather than a complaint about one vendor: Elicit's own published accuracy figures are inconsistent across its own pages - 96% and 99.4% for extraction, 99.5% and 97/99% for screening. Any of those numbers might be right. None of them arrives with a methodology, a corpus or a definition of what counted as correct, and they do not agree with each other. That is not a reason to avoid the tool. It is a reason your confidence in it should come from your own sample-check on your own pile, which takes twenty minutes and produces a number you can actually cite.

Part 3 · integrity checks

Retraction checking, and why there is no excuse 6 min live

A retracted paper does not disappear. It keeps its DOI, keeps sitting in Google Scholar, keeps being cited, and reads exactly like a valid paper - which means it will walk into your extraction table on the strength of a good abstract. The check that catches it is close to free. Crossref holds 185,277,841 records, needs no sign-up, and carries retraction and post-publication update information: hand it a DOI and it will tell you whether the record has been withdrawn or corrected. Adding a mailto to your request moves you into the polite pool and triples your rate limit from 1 request per second to 3. At that rate a 200-source library is checked in under two minutes.

LiveThe check, and where it goes in the workflow4 min▶

Run it twice: once when a source clears full-text screening, and once again the week you ship. Retractions land months and years after publication, so a library that was clean in March is not automatically clean in August, and the memo carries the date you checked.

  • Collect the DOIs for everything in your included set. A source with no DOI is not exempt, it is harder - check the journal's own page for a notice.
  • Query Crossref per DOI and read the update information: retractions, corrections, expressions of concern. All three change how much weight a source can carry, not just retraction.
  • Record the check in your log with the date. "Retraction-checked 2026-08-08 via Crossref" is one line and it answers a question a reviewer would otherwise have to ask.
  • Re-check before you ship, and re-check anything a claim depends on heavily. The load-bearing sources deserve the extra minute.

Then say the quiet part out loud: because this check costs minutes and needs no account, not having done it is a choice. It is the single easiest thing in this entire track to be caught not doing.

Real world

Guo X, Dong L, Hao D, Front Cell Dev Biol 11:1339390 was retracted on 2024-02-16 (retraction notice DOI 10.3389/fcell.2024.1386861) over AI-generated figures. The detail that makes this the right case for this session: the AI use was disclosed. The authors followed the rule. It still failed, because disclosure is not review and nobody checked what the tool produced before it went into the paper. Declaring that you used a tool tells a reader where to look; it does not tell them the output is correct.

And the reason a retraction check is not the same as being safe: Glynn (arXiv:2411.15218) found 768 documents with suspected undeclared AI use, of which only 4.3% were altered post-publication. Detection is not correction. The overwhelming majority of flagged material stayed exactly as published - so the record you are about to cite may be quietly wrong while carrying no notice at all. Your retraction check catches the cases the system has acted on. Source tiering, population checks and the contradiction hunt are what cover the rest.

Demo · the anti-lever

Forty more sources, one fewer claim ★ 14 min · everyone runs it

This is the session that owns the anti-lever, so we run it properly rather than as a party trick. The lab opens with five levers on and the audit at its best: 9 of 10 claims surviving, 22 sources read. Then you add 40 sources in bulk - the reflexive move, the one that feels like diligence - and watch the pile go to 62 while surviving claims drop to 8. One of the 40 is retracted, and it lands in the support for a claim that was previously clean. Nearly three times the reading, one claim worse off, and a memo that is now easier to break.

Start where b1 left you: five levers on, 9 of 10 surviving, 22 sources. Note which claim is the honest unknown and leave it alone - it is not what changes here.

Before clicking, predict. Write down whether 40 more sources will help, do nothing, or hurt. Most people say small gain.

Press Add 40 sources. Sources read jumps to 62; surviving claims fall to 8. Find the claim that died and read its failure rule - it now rests partly on a retracted source.

Now the harder half. Leave the 40 in and turn Source tiering off. Feel the difference: untiered breadth is worse than untiered narrowness, because every extra unscreened hit is another chance to import something retracted, duplicated, promotional or off-population.

Turn tiering back on, take the 40 back out, and say the rule in your own words to the person next to you: breadth is a multiplier on your screening discipline, not a substitute for it.

What is real here and what is a teaching corpus The 22 sources - and the 40 bulk hits - are a teaching corpus built for one decision, not a real literature. Everything else is real: each source carries a type, tier, year, sample size and funder, and the audit is a rule engine that reads those attributes in the fixed order you learned in b1. The claim that dies when you add 40 sources dies because a specific retracted record enters its support, and you can trace it. The rules are the transferable part - the corpus is the sandbox.
Homework

Try it yourself - this week ◐ 35-45 min total

  • Write the six-line rule set for a question you are working on right now, before you search again. Include a reason for the date window. Then search, and count how many borderline calls the rules made for you.
  • Build the log: found, deduped, screened, excluded-with-reason, retrieved, assessed, included. Make the numbers add up. If you cannot reconstruct them for a piece of work you already shipped, that is the finding.
  • Take the reference list of your last deck or report and retraction-check every DOI through Crossref. Record the date you checked. Tell us next session if you found anything.
  • Run the anti-lever from a clean 9 of 10, then turn Source tiering off with the 40 still in. Write two sentences a manager would accept explaining why "let's look at more sources" was the wrong instruction.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · What are the three bands of the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram?

The 2009 diagram had a separate Eligibility band; PRISMA 2020 removed it, and "reports assessed for eligibility" now sits inside Screening. Three bands, and remember PRISMA is a reporting guideline - it tells you what to disclose, not whether a study is any good.

2 · From a clean 9 of 10, adding 40 unscreened sources drops the audit to 8. What actually happened?

The 40 bulk hits are all tier 4 and one of them is retracted. The audit does not care how much you read, only what your claims rest on - so an unscreened pile is a delivery mechanism for retracted, duplicated, promotional and off-population material. Breadth becomes useful only after tiering and screening exist.

3 · Why is skipping a retraction check hard to defend?

Retracted work keeps its DOI and keeps being findable and cited - nobody tells you. The check is close to free, which is exactly why not doing it reads as a choice. Note the limit too: Glynn found only 4.3% of 768 documents with suspected undeclared AI use were altered post-publication, so detection is not correction.
